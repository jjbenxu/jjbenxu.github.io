Featured project

What actually determines an NBA player's salary?

Excel · Regression, inference, probability modeling · Summer 2026

A five-stage statistical analysis of 513 NBA players from the 2020–21 season, built as a team project across five graduate assignments — from cleaning the raw export through to a validated multiple regression model.

9models tested

Context

Salary is the single largest constraint a front office manages, and the league's pay structure is famously top-heavy. We asked a plain question — which performance measures actually move a player's salary — and treated it the way a commercial team would treat a pricing problem.

My role

I carried the analysis end to end — cleaning and consolidating the raw export, engineering the two advanced measures, testing the distributional assumptions, running the hypothesis tests and confidence intervals, and building the regression through backward elimination. I also wrote the commentary documenting the reasoning at each stage. This was a team assignment and the interpretation was worked through together; my teammates pushed back on conclusions and that scrutiny made the final model better, particularly the decision to drop effective field goal percentage.

Approach

Cleaned the raw export down to twelve core box-score fields and engineered two advanced measures — effective field goal percentage and assist-to-turnover ratio. Tested distributional assumptions, ran one-sample t-tests and confidence intervals, then built a multiple regression and reduced it by backward elimination across nine successive models.

Outcome

A two-variable model — age and total points — explaining 50.2% of salary variance across all 513 players (F = 257.6). Each additional year of age predicted +$633,654; each additional point scored predicted +$13,308.

The decision I'd want to be asked about in an interview

Effective field goal percentage was a statistically significant predictor, and I removed it anyway. Its coefficient was strongly negative — the model was claiming that better shooters earn less, which is nonsense. The cause was sample size: players with almost no minutes attempt almost no shots, so a handful of makes produces an inflated percentage. Keeping a significant-but-senseless predictor would have made the model look stronger on paper and worse in practice. The final model kept only the two variables that were both statistically and practically defensible, and R² held near 0.50 the entire way down.

Finding — probability modeling

Of eight continuous variables, only age was near-symmetric enough to justify a normal approximation. Asked how often a player earns more than $10 million, the normal model returned 37.1% against an empirical 22.2%. A front office sizing a free-agent budget off the normal figure would have planned for eight-figure competition on roughly two-thirds more targets than actually existed — and over-reserved cap space accordingly.

Finding — inference

Mean salary of $7,027,351 tested against a claimed $6M: rejected at α = 0.05 (t = 2.571, df = 512, one-tailed p = 0.005). But the scouting benchmark of 500 points survived (t = −1.613, p = 0.107) — the shortfall sat inside ordinary sampling variation. The 95% interval on mean salary, scaled to a 15-man roster, spans $93.6M to $117.2M: a $23.5M planning band.

The full analysis — every workbook, plus a dated decision log recording the analytical choices behind each stage — is maintained in a version-controlled repository, in the same way an analytics team would document work for review.

04 — Market Perspective

How I read the market

Three things I pay attention to in healthcare, pharma, and financial services — and why the NBA project above is more relevant to them than it first appears.

Trend

Commercial teams are being asked to quantify what they used to argue

What's changing

In both of my internships, positioning decisions were made from experience and customer conversation. That is steadily being replaced by demands for evidence — market access and payer negotiations in particular now turn on defensible quantitative cases. The people who can hold both halves of that conversation are the ones commercial teams are short of.

Challenge

Skewed distributions break the models built on top of them

What's hard about it

Healthcare cost data, drug revenue, and financial returns share a shape: heavily right-skewed, with the decisions that matter living in the tail. My own analysis produced a clean demonstration — a normal approximation overstated a tail probability by two-thirds. The same error in a cost forecast or a risk model is not an academic problem; it is a mispriced reserve.

Where I fit

The judgment call is the differentiator, not the model

My angle

Software will fit a regression. What it will not do is notice that a significant coefficient implies something absurd about the world and remove it anyway. Domain knowledge is what turns a fitted model into a trustworthy one — and a health science degree plus two summers inside pharma commercial teams is precisely the domain knowledge these industries are hiring for.
